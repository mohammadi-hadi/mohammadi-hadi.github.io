The AI layer behind an academic job board

Single sign-on in front of independent modules, 18,002 jobs scored, and two thirds of the pipeline’s model spend going to one function nobody would guess.

DRAFT — unpublished7 min read

Most of what I write here is about measurement. This one is about the thing being measured: the AI stack behind an academic recruitment platform used by Dutch universities and university medical centres, which I lead the technical side of. Colleagues ask what it actually looks like, and the honest answer is less exotic and more operational than the phrase “AI layer” suggests.

The shape of it

There is no monolith. There is a portal and a set of independent modules behind it.

The portal does single sign-on over OIDC, and on success issues an RS256-signed session cookie. Every module verifies that cookie locally against the public key, so a module never calls the portal to ask who someone is. Per-user module grants live in Postgres, so access to the CV sorter and access to the vacancy rewriter are separate decisions. The modules themselves handle CV extraction and de-identification, concept extraction, candidate-job matching, vacancy rewriting, and the dashboards over the top.

The reason for that shape is not architectural taste. These modules were built at different times, some are experiments that may not survive the year, and they have different appetites for risk. Local cookie verification means a module can be taken down, rebuilt or thrown away without touching authentication, and a failing experiment fails alone.

What counts as scale here

Roughly 18,002 open positions in a month of comparison data, and 3.2 million candidate-job scores computed across them. That is a small number by internet standards and a large one for the decisions attached to it: each score is part of a shortlist a hiring committee reads, and there are only so many academic jobs in a country.

Which is why the interesting engineering constraint is not throughput. It is that every unit of work is attached to a person applying for a job, and a system that is 99% right is wrong about a career several times a week.

Where the money goes, which is not where you would guess

Per-call cost tracking by model and by function, over one month, produced the only genuinely surprising operational result I have had from this system.

Two thirds of the pipeline’s spend went to de-identification. Stripping names, emails and phone numbers out of CVs before anything else touches them took 65% of what the extraction pipeline billed in the month I measured. Reading the PDFs took another 31%. Everything I would have guessed the money was going to, matching and classification together, came to under 4%. The actual intelligence, the part anyone would describe as the AI, was a rounding error on the bill.

In hindsight it is obvious. Matching runs on structured text, and getting to structured text means reading every page of every CV with a model. The lesson I took is that the cost profile of an LLM system is usually dominated by whatever has to touch the raw input, and that this is invisible until you break the bill down by function instead of by model. Breaking it down by model tells you which vendor to negotiate with. Breaking it down by function tells you what to go and fix.

Latency, and one place we stopped calling a model

Scoring a pool of 300 CVs against one vacancy with an LLM takes a few minutes and several hundred thousand tokens. A local cross-encoder does the same ranking in about a quarter of a second per CV, on a laptop, with no API call at all.

The evaluation said the two rank the pool about equally well for this task. So the default is the local model, and the LLM is reserved for the parts that need reading comprehension. That decision came out of measurement and could not have come out of anywhere else: both options looked fine in a demo.

What runs before a release

The failure mode I care most about is silent: a module returns a plausible shortlist built from a stale index, or a rewritten vacancy that quietly drops a requirement, and nothing anywhere goes red.

So a release test walks one real vacancy through the whole chain, draft to rewritten to scored to published, across all four systems, and checks the result at each boundary. Alongside it are several dozen scripted checks over the portal itself: every module tile reachable for a user with the right grant, and unreachable for one without. The most recent full pass cleared every portal tile and every scripted check, and confirmed that the rewriter preserved each checked fact from the original posting.

That last one is the check I would keep if I could only keep one. A vacancy rewriter that improves the prose and drops the salary band is worse than no rewriter, and it is exactly the kind of failure that looks like success in every screenshot.

What is measured, and what is not

The evaluation work behind this stack has its own write-ups: two matching systems agreeing on 3.6% of top candidates, and an agreement metric that moved while the model stood still. Both came out of trying to answer a straightforward-sounding question about which system to keep.

What is not measured yet is the thing everyone asks first: how much time this saves a hiring committee. There are internal figures for that, and I am deliberately not quoting them here, because the study that would support them properly is still running. Two experienced academic reviewers are working through the same invented CVs the tool was evaluated on, so that the comparison is against human judgement on identical material instead of against a recollection of how long screening used to take.

Until that lands, the defensible claims are the ones with a measurement behind them: what the ranking does, what it costs, and what it gets wrong. The time saving is the interesting number and it is the one I have the least evidence for, which is the reverse of how these systems are usually described.

Written at an architectural level: no internal system names, no customer data, and cost given as shares of spend. The evaluation datasets referenced are synthetic. The evaluation methods are packaged as open-source audit libraries.
